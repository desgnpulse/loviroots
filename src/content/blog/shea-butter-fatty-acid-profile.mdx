Most plant oils sit on top of your skin. Shea butter does not. The reason is not how thick it is.

Shea butter contains a specific combination of fatty acids that your skin already uses to build its own barrier. That structural match is why shea integrates with skin tissue rather than forming a separate layer over it.

---

Shea butter is approximately 50% saturated fat and 50% unsaturated fat. That ratio sets it apart from most plant-derived fats.

The two dominant fatty acids are stearic acid and oleic acid. A typical shea butter from West or East Africa runs roughly 40–55% stearic acid and 34–40% oleic acid. Linoleic acid and palmitic acid make up most of the remainder.

Each one has a distinct role.

---

Stearic acid is a saturated fat your skin produces directly. It is one of the main lipids in the stratum corneum. That is the outermost skin layer, the one that regulates moisture and keeps irritants out.

Most plant oils lack meaningful stearic acid. Coconut oil is predominantly lauric acid, a fat that does not occur naturally in human skin. Shea is different. Its stearic content puts it structurally closer to skin than almost any other plant fat.

When shea butter delivers stearic acid to dry or compromised skin, something specific happens. The skin gets a raw material it already uses to repair its barrier. That is different from coating the surface with something foreign.

Oleic acid behaves differently. It is unsaturated and penetrates deeper into the skin than stearic acid. It also acts as a carrier, increasing how much of what you apply alongside it your skin actually absorbs. Where stearic acid rebuilds the outer surface, oleic acid works below it.

---

Linoleic acid is the fatty acid most people skip past in discussions about shea. For reactive or compromised skin, it may be the most important one.

Linoleic acid is an omega-6 fatty acid classified as essential. Your body cannot produce it. You have to supply it. The skin uses linoleic acid to synthesise ceramides, the lipid molecules that hold the stratum corneum together. A deficiency of linoleic acid in the outer skin layer is directly linked to impaired barrier function. Cracked skin, persistent dryness, and sensitivity to products can all trace back to this.

Shea butter supplies linoleic acid in a form the skin can absorb. It is not the highest linoleic source available. But it delivers linoleic acid alongside stearic and oleic acid, in proportions your skin already uses.

---

The term "karite acids" comes from the Fulani word for the shea tree. It refers to the fatty acid fraction of shea butter, dominated by stearic acid. That makes it unusual among plant fats.

The high stearic content is why shea is solid at room temperature. Unlike lighter plant oils, shea does not melt in your palm before reaching your skin. Its melting point sits close to body temperature. It softens on contact, not before. That matters for how evenly it spreads and how effectively it deposits the fatty acids where your skin needs them.

---

There is an additional fraction in unrefined shea butter that the fatty acid profile does not capture. It is called the unsaponifiable fraction. It includes triterpene alcohols, cinnamic acid esters, and tocopherols.

These compounds are removed during industrial refining. They are what give raw shea its distinctive smell and yellow colour. They also give unrefined shea mild anti-inflammatory activity. Research documents this through the COX and LOX enzyme pathways, the same ones involved in skin redness and swelling.

Refined shea retains the fatty acid profile largely intact. The unsaponifiable fraction is significantly reduced or absent.

---

What this means in practice.

If you are buying shea for texture and moisture retention, refined shea will perform. If you are buying for reactive or sensitive skin, you need both: the fatty acids and the unsaponifiable fraction.

Check the colour when you buy. Unrefined shea is ivory to pale yellow. White shea has been bleached. Check the smell. Raw shea has a faint earthy, nutty scent. No scent means the deodorization step has run, which also removes the unsaponifiable fraction.

The fatty acid profile makes shea butter compatible with skin. The unsaponifiable fraction is what makes it active. You want both.
