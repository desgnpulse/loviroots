Your skin barrier makes its own fats. Shea butter has a fatty acid profile that closely matches them. Cocoa butter does not.

That is not a minor detail. It explains why the two butters feel similar in your hand but behave differently once they are on your skin.

---

Oleic acid is the dominant fat in shea butter. It makes up roughly 45 percent of its fatty acid content. Oleic acid is a monounsaturated fat with a relatively small molecular structure. That small size lets it pass through the outermost layer of your skin and reach deeper into the epidermis.

Shea also contains linoleic acid, at about 15 percent. Linoleic acid is a building block your skin uses to produce ceramides. Ceramides are the mortar in your skin barrier. When your skin runs low on them, moisture escapes faster and irritants enter more easily.

Cocoa butter reads differently. Its dominant fatty acids are stearic acid and palmitic acid, both heavily saturated. Those molecules are denser and do not travel far. They remain near the surface and form a firm, protective film.

That film serves a purpose. It slows the rate at which water evaporates from your skin. Dermatologists call this occlusion. Cocoa butter does it well, and in very dry conditions, an occlusive layer is exactly what your skin needs.

But occlusion is not the same as nourishment. A sealed skin barrier and a nourished skin barrier are two different states.

---

Shea butter has one more property that separates it from almost every other plant fat. It contains between 7 and 10 percent unsaponifiables. Most plant-based oils contain less than 1 percent.

Unsaponifiables are compounds that cannot be broken down in the standard fat-processing reaction. In shea butter, they include triterpenes and phytosterols. These are the biologically active fraction. They are what makes shea butter behave more like a skin treatment than a simple fat.

The phytosterols in shea butter suppress NF-kB signalling. NF-kB is the body's master switch for inflammatory response. When it activates repeatedly, your skin barrier becomes more permeable. Moisture exits faster. Your skin becomes more reactive over time.

Shea butter's unsaponifiable fraction interrupts that process. Applied consistently, it helps your skin hold onto its own regulatory chemistry rather than fighting against daily irritants.

A 2024 in-vitro study found that shea butter reduced transepidermal water loss by 37.8 percent within 24 hours. Skin hydration increased by 58 percent in the same window. Cocoa butter builds a surface barrier. Shea butter works at the level where those results happen.

---

What does this mean for your routine?

If your skin is acutely dry and you need to lock in surface moisture quickly, cocoa butter does that job. Apply it immediately after a shower while your skin is still slightly damp. The occlusive film traps the moisture underneath.

If your skin stays dry no matter what you put on it, that is a different problem. It is a barrier function issue, not a hydration issue. Shea butter addresses the reasons your skin keeps losing moisture rather than just sealing over the result.

For dark skin specifically, there is one more thing to consider. Cocoa butter is rated low on the comedogenic scale, but it does sit on the skin's surface. If you are dealing with hyperpigmentation, that occlusive film keeps post-inflammatory pigment sealed at the surface. It does not contribute to its reduction. Shea butter is anti-inflammatory. It does not treat dark spots directly, but it does not add to the conditions that make them worse.

In Nairobi humidity, shea butter's semi-occlusive nature makes a practical difference. It forms a breathable layer rather than a full seal. Fully occlusive products can feel heavy and sticky by midday, particularly in the rainy season.

---

Try this: the next time you apply either butter, press it into your skin and wait 10 minutes. Shea butter will mostly disappear. Cocoa butter will leave a noticeable film. That film tells you what job the product is doing. Both are useful. But if your skin stays dry after weeks of application, the problem may be your choice. You may be reaching for the sealer when you need the feeder.

Shea butter feeds your skin barrier. Cocoa butter seals over it. In most cases, you need to repair the structure before you seal it.
