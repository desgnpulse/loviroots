Archaeologists have documented shea butter use in Burkina Faso since 100 CE. European beauty companies only started adding it to their formulations about 30 years ago. Those 1,900 years in between were not a gap in history. They are where the knowledge lived.

The shea tree, Vitellaria paradoxa, grows across a belt stretching from Senegal to Uganda. That is 13 countries. Women across that belt have been collecting its nuts, roasting them, grinding them, and churning out the fat for generations. This was not a simple beauty routine. It was a system of knowledge passed between women, tested across seasons, and built over centuries.

---

In West Africa, infants were massaged with shea butter from birth. Women applied it to lips and skin before the Sahel's dry season. Minor wounds and burns were dressed with it. Hair was coated to prevent breakage from dry air. The same ingredient, applied to different conditions, for one consistent outcome: protection.

What those women understood through practice, modern science now explains through mechanism.

Shea butter contains triterpenes, specifically lupeol, butyrospermol, and parkiol. These compounds actively reduce inflammation at the cellular level. Research published in the Journal of Complementary and Integrative Medicine confirmed this. Shea extract significantly reduced TNF-alpha and interleukin-1 beta markers in test tissue. Those are the same inflammatory markers your skin raises when it faces wind, dry air, UV exposure, or irritation.

The fatty acids in shea are absorbed directly into the stratum corneum, the outermost layer of your skin. They replace the lipids your skin loses through dry air, hard water, and daily washing. One study measured a 37.8% reduction in transepidermal water loss within 24 hours of application. Skin hydration increased by 58% in the same window.

That is why the infant skin practice worked. It is why wind-burned lips healed. It was not tradition for tradition's sake. It was an accurate read on how skin works, applied consistently over a thousand years.

---

The historical record gives the timeline. The 14th-century Moroccan scholar Ibn Battuta passed through the Mali Empire around 1354. He recorded shea butter as a valued trade commodity in the region's economy. By 1796, the Scottish explorer Mungo Park documented how locals extracted butter from the shea nut. The knowledge was fully established by then. It had been there long before either man arrived to write about it.

Shea production was, and still is, structurally a women's industry. In communities across the shea belt, collecting the nuts and processing them into butter was women's work. The income went directly to women. Burkina Faso and Ghana together supply 60 to 75 percent of all traded shea globally. Female-run cooperatives process the majority of what reaches international markets.

This matters for a reason beyond the economic one. Knowledge that stays within a single community for over a thousand years tends to be knowledge that actually works. What did not work was discarded. What survived was used again.

---

When you use shea butter, you are using something with an unusual track record. Over 1,000 years of daily use by millions of women. On textured, melanin-rich skin. In a high-UV, low-humidity climate. That is not a small data set.

There is one question worth asking when you buy shea butter. Is it the same thing those women were using?

Refined shea, the kind found in most mass-market products, has been stripped of its triterpenes through high-heat processing. What you are left with is the fatty acid profile alone. The anti-inflammatory activity, the UV mitigation, the wound-healing properties, all of that came from what was removed.

The women in Burkina Faso were not refining their shea. Think about what that tells you.
